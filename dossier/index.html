Chapter 01 The filing

This is not a proposal. It is an application in progress.

Part One of the horizontal expansion permit has been submitted. The geotechnical investigation is ongoing and is expected to continue into early 2027, with water quality work to follow. All Army Corps of Engineers jurisdictional requirements are complete, and the hydrogeologic work plan already given to the Commission supports the Part Two submission.

"On the horizontal expansion permit, Part One has been submitted; the geotechnical investigation is ongoing and expected to continue into early 2027, with water quality work to follow. All Army Corps of Engineers jurisdictional requirements have been completed, with no land-development obstacles identified." Chapter 02 The approach

It is easier to argue about a landfill than to look at one. So look at it.

East Tennessee. The Valley and Ridge, folded northeast to southwest, and the river running through it.

Loudon County. The Tennessee River bends west around a peninsula. The pale ground on that peninsula is the landfill.

This is the same ground photographed from orbit over twelve years. Watch the pale area, and watch the trees between it and the water.

The lined cell and the pond visible in 2014 are gone — buried under a single continuous mass that spreads south and west.

South and west is the river. The expansion now filed would extend that same movement, in that same direction.

Chapter 02 What is next to it

Look at the map behind this text. The pale terraced ground is the landfill.

The orange mesh pressing against it on the north and east is not forest. It is subdivided residential lots, street by street, already platted.

On the west, the Tennessee River. This is the shape of the decision: a Class I landfill with a river on one side and neighbourhoods on the other, applying to grow sideways.

The expansion permit does not need anyone's vote to be filed. It is filed.

Chapter 03 The eastern slope

The inspector came in July. He came back in August.

TDEC issued Areas of Concern for exposed waste and for flagging along the east slope, both tied to the availability of soil. The landfill manager submitted these photographs of that slope into the public record on September 2, 2026.

Cover soil is the whole dispute. The site hauls roughly 400 to 600 cubic yards of soil a week for daily cover, but that hauled material cannot legally be used for final cover, which requires a certified two-foot compacted layer, geosynthetics and a further foot of topsoil. The on-site borrow soil that does conform is not accessible pending a grading permit.

"the site is currently hauling approximately 400–600 cubic yards of soil per week for daily cover. He clarified that this hauled material cannot be used for intermediate or final cover on capped slopes, which require a certified two-foot compacted conformance layer, geosynthetics, and an additional foot of topsoil meeting TDEC specifications." Engineering report, minutes of the August 20, 2026 meeting, September 17, 2026 packet, p. 8.

Chapter 05 What you can actually do

Three things, and none of them require a lawyer.

01 — Show up

Go to the next Commission meeting

Public comment happens at the start. The county publishes the schedule. The room is small, and attendance is visible in the record.

 02 — Ask

File a public records request

Tennessee law gives you the right to inspect these records. The Commission's records officer post is currently vacant, so send requests to the chair and copy the county open records office.

 03 — Write

Submit written public comment

Written comment goes into the packet and becomes part of the permanent record, whether or not you can attend in person.
